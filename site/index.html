A five-year stock & options challenge

Five years. One stock. Its options.
No hindsight.

You get $100,000 and a stock drawn at random from real market history. Its name and dates are hidden and the price starts at 100, so you can't trade on what you remember. Each turn is one month: set your stock position, long or short, and trade one-month calls and puts. At the end the stock is revealed and you're scored on Sharpe ratio, maximum drawdown and IRR against six benchmark strategies played on the same data.

      How it works

      Blind Tape is a trading game built on real daily market data. The rules are simple enough to learn in a minute, and the scoring uses the same risk-adjusted metrics a portfolio manager would be judged on.

      The game

      • Stake: $100,000, fixed. No deposits or withdrawals.
      • The stock: drawn at random from a pool of stocks with very different histories (moonshots, decliners, cyclicals, defensives, and the S&P 500 itself). In blind mode the ticker and calendar dates are hidden and the price is rebased to 100 on day one; returns are unchanged.
      • Window: the full challenge covers the most recent 60 months (1,260 trading days) ending at the latest close in the data. A quick round is a 12-month slice of that window. Each turn is exactly 21 trading days.
      • Stock position: set as a percent of equity, from −100% (short) to +150% (long on margin). Stock trades cost 5 basis points. If you don't touch the slider, your share count is unchanged.
      • Options: European calls and puts at 90/95/100/105/110% of the current price, expiring at the end of the month (21 trading days), cash-settled at intrinsic value. Size is a percent of equity notional (e.g. "size 100%" = options on as many shares as your equity would buy). Up to four legs per month, and at most 200% of equity notional across all legs.
      • Leverage cap includes options: your net exposure, meaning the stock plus the delta of every option leg, must stay between −100% and +150% when you place an order. Options can't be used to get around the stock limits.
      • Writing options: written calls must be covered by long shares. Written puts must be cash-secured: after the whole order you need cash at least equal to the strike value, not counting short-sale proceeds.
      • Financing: idle cash (including short-sale proceeds) earns the 13-week T-bill yield (^IRX) daily. Margin debit pays T-bill + 1.5%. Short positions pay a 0.5%/yr borrow fee. An order can't take borrowing above 50% of equity.
      • Drift and risk controls: the limits above apply to orders. Between orders, positions drift with the market. You can always keep a drifted position or bring the stock back within the limits, but buying options still needs buying power. The account is marked to market at every daily close, including option-expiry days. If equity falls below 25% of the gross stock position, a margin call liquidates the stock and closes all options. If equity hits zero, the game is over, the rest of the window counts as zero, and the account ranks last.

      Option pricing

      Historical option quotes for every stock aren't freely available, so options are priced with Black-Scholes on an implied volatility built from market data on each day:

      IVatm² = RV63(stock)² + [ (0.88 × VIX/100)² − RV63(S&P 500)² ]

      IV(K) = IVatm × (1 − skew × ln(K/S))

      RV63 is trailing 63-day realized volatility. The bracket is the market's variance risk premium on that day: how much option buyers pay for S&P 500 volatility above what the index has actually been delivering. The VIX is a variance-swap level that includes the skew, so at-the-money index vol is taken as 0.88 × VIX. Adding the premium once, in variance units, prices at-the-money SPY options at 0.88 × VIX, and gives single stocks a small premium over their own realized vol. That is consistent with research showing the volatility risk premium is concentrated in index options. A smile is then applied across strikes. For the index the skew is steep and runs one way (slope 3.0), so puts are richer and calls cheaper. For single stocks the puts get a milder skew (1.25) and the calls stay at at-the-money vol, because out-of-the-money stock calls aren't cheap in real markets. Each smile is clipped to between 0.5× and 2× at-the-money vol. At-the-money vol is floored at 75% of the stock's realized vol, and every IV is kept between 8% and 250%. You buy at the ask and sell at the bid: a half-spread of 2.5% of the premium, with a minimum of 5 bp of the stock price. The risk-free rate is the T-bill yield. Because the price series is total-return adjusted, the model treats the stock as non-dividend-paying.

      How we got here. Each version below was replaced after a reality check on real data.

      1. Multiplying each stock's realized vol by the index's VIX-to-realized ratio made covered calls beat buy-and-hold on almost every stock.
      2. Pricing every strike at the VIX level let a 5%-out-of-the-money SPY covered call beat SPY by 46 percentage points over five years.
      3. Applying the index-style smile to single stocks made their out-of-the-money calls too cheap, so call buyers profited even after delta hedging.

      With the current model, delta-hedged buyers of out-of-the-money single-stock options lose on average, as the empirical evidence says they should. The SPY 105% buy-write finishes within about 5 points of SPY over the full window (+83% vs +78%), down from 46.

      Scoring

      • Sharpe ratio: mean daily return in excess of the T-bill rate ÷ its standard deviation, × √252.
      • Sortino ratio: the same excess return ÷ downside deviation, × √252.
      • Maximum drawdown: the largest peak-to-trough decline of the daily equity curve.
      • IRR: the money-weighted internal rate of return (XIRR, actual/365) of the account's cash flows. With a fixed stake there are only two flows (the $100,000 in and the ending value out), so IRR equals the compound annual growth rate. The engine computes a general XIRR so contributions could be added later.
      • Beta: covariance of your daily returns with SPY's, ÷ SPY's variance.
      • Return per unit of drawdown: IRR in excess of the T-bill return ÷ maximum drawdown (a Calmar-style ratio), with the drawdown floored at 1%.
      • Overall rank: the assignment judges on Sharpe, maximum drawdown and IRR. Every strategy is ranked on Sharpe, on return per unit of drawdown, and on IRR, and ordered by the average of the three ranks; exact ties share a rank. Max drawdown enters per unit of return, not raw. Raw drawdown shrinks with position size while Sharpe doesn't, so a tiny near-cash position (say, writing a few puts) would otherwise collect two good ranks for free. Sharpe alone also misorders some cases: a near-riskless account that trails T-bills slightly has a tiny negative excess return divided by a tiny volatility. A busted account has no meaningful Sharpe and ranks last.

      Benchmarks

      Every game is also played automatically by six benchmark strategies on the same window, with the same costs: buy & hold the stock; buy & hold the S&P 500 (SPY); a covered call (long 100%, write 105% calls monthly); a protective put (long 100%, buy 95% puts monthly); a 200-day trend rule (long 100% when the price is above its 200-day average, otherwise T-bills); and T-bills only.

      Data & updates

      Daily dividend- and split-adjusted closes for every stock, SPY, the VIX and the 13-week T-bill yield are downloaded by a scheduled GitHub Actions job every weekday after the US close. The job validates each series (no gaps or non-finite values, plausible ranges, enough history), commits only when the data changed, and redeploys the site. Because the game window always ends at the latest close, it moves forward automatically as new data arrives. Adding a stock means adding one line to config/tickers.json: the next run downloads its full history with no code changes. See the Data page for the current status.

      Verification

      The engine has unit tests: Black-Scholes against textbook values and put-call parity, margin calls, short squeezes, option settlement, the covered and cash-secured rules, and the Sharpe/Sortino/drawdown/XIRR math. A separate Python script independently recomputes every metric from the real equity curves of all benchmark strategies on all stocks, using pandas, and checks it against the browser's numbers.

      Limitations

      • Option prices are model prices, not historical quotes. The smile is a simple two-parameter shape (one skew for the index, one for all single stocks), not fitted to each stock's real option market, and earnings-day volatility isn't modelled.
      • Trades execute at the daily close. There are no intraday stops, and decisions are monthly.
      • Fractional shares are allowed, and the market impact of trades is ignored beyond the fixed costs.
      • Pre-start history shown on the chart starts up to a year before the game begins. In blind mode the stock's identity can sometimes be inferred from the shape of its chart, or from the era revealed by the T-bill rate. Blind mode is honour-based: a static site has to download the price file, so a player who opens the browser's developer tools can see it.
      • Replay links reproduce a game exactly on the same data snapshot. When the data provider revises adjusted prices (e.g. after a dividend), a replay can differ by a few dollars.

      How updates work

      A GitHub Actions workflow (.github/workflows/update-data.yml) runs every weekday at 22:30 UTC (4:30pm CST), when the config changes, or on demand. It tries yfinance, then the Yahoo Finance chart API, then Nasdaq's historical API (and FRED for T-bills), validates every series, commits only changed files, and redeploys this site. If a source fails, the last good data is kept and this page flags it as stale.
